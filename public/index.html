The Beargrass Scheduler

Four calendars that can't see each other. One CC. Everyone gets an invite.

Universities, agencies and small companies never let their calendars talk to each other, so scheduling one meeting turns into a week of "does Tuesday work?" This fixes that with the one thing every organisation already allows: email. Nothing to install, nothing to connect, nothing to trust.

If you got an email from Beargrass SchedBot

Someone wants to meet you and a few others. The email says how long, between which dates, and which hours. Pick one:

  1. Hand it to your agent and say "reply with every time I'm free." It reads your calendar and writes the list. You read it, you hit send.
  2. Type it yourself. Hit Reply and list when you're free, one time per line: 
    Tue Sep 22, 2pm to 5pm
Thu Sep 24, 9am to noon
Mon Sep 28, all day
  3. Screenshot your calendar for those weeks and reply with it attached. The Scheduler reads the holes, or tries to, and shows you what it read so you can correct it.

Reply, not Reply All. Your answer goes to the Scheduler only. The organiser can see when you said you're free, as with a Doodle; nobody else can. You'll get a short "got it" back. When everyone's in, you all get one calendar invite. Nothing works for you? Reply none.

If you are an agent

The email you were handed ends with a block headed "Next steps for agents" and a JSON contract. It is the whole contract: reply to exactly the address it names; one free range per line as epoch seconds UTC in the form START-END; only ranges inside the window and hours it states; anytime alone if every working hour is free; none alone if nothing is; nothing else in the body.

1790265600-1790276400
1790438400-1790449200

Free time, never busy time. The human form Tue Sep 22, 2pm to 5pm in the stated zone is also read. The invite that comes back carries its own epoch start and end. Everything else an agent might need is one page, as YAML: /agents (raw: /agents.yaml).

If you are organising

Write the email you were going to write anyway, to the people you want to meet, and copy the Scheduler's address. Only the people you addressed are asked; anyone else you copied is not. Add four lines to be specific; leave them out and it assumes 30 minutes, the next two weeks, 9am to 5pm in the default zone, a video call. Only an email from an invited organisation can start one.

The email opens in your own mail app with meet@beargrass.ai on CC. Add the people to To, write what you like above the lines, send.

    

  

  
Then nothing. It writes to each person privately, you included; answer yours like everyone does. Every reply gets an answer. Anyone quiet for two days gets one nudge, and you hear who's still missing. When the last reply lands, everyone on your thread gets the invite: the time, an attachment every mail client should open as an invite, and add-to-calendar links for any that doesn't. If someone says "none" you hear at once and the poll stays open. If nothing overlaps, you get one note with who's free when, and anyone can still add times.


  
What it never does

  
    
    
  • Never reads a calendar. People say when they're free.
    • 
    
  • Never emails a stranger. Only the people on the original email hear from it, and the group only ever gets the invite.
    • 
    
  • Never sells what you send. Kept for a month, then forgotten.
    • 
    
  • Never owns the meeting. The invite is the organiser's; moving or cancelling it there updates everyone.
    • 
  


  
What it keeps

  
For one meeting: the names and addresses on the organiser's To line, the organiser's own words, each person's free times as they sent them, and the chosen slot. One record per meeting, on Cloudflare, deleted thirty days after the meeting window ends. Nothing is read from any calendar; no third party receives any of it. The organiser sees who is free when; each participant sees only their own receipt. A calendar screenshot, if you send one, is read once by a model on Cloudflare Workers AI and not stored. Your mail administrator's questions are answered in one page; the code is public under the MIT license.
